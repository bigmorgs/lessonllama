# How to Plan a Week of Lessons in One Sitting (and Actually Keep Your Sunday)

You know the feeling. It's Sunday afternoon, the weekend is slipping away, and somewhere between folding laundry and pretending to relax, a low hum of dread kicks in: *I still haven't planned next week.*

If lesson planning regularly eats your evenings and weekends, the problem usually isn't that you're slow or disorganized. It's that most of us were never actually taught a planning *system*. We just… sit down and hope it happens.

Here's the good news: with a few structural changes, most teachers can map out a solid week of lessons in one focused sitting — 60 to 90 minutes — and walk away with their Sunday intact. Let's break down how.

## Separate Planning From Prepping

This is the single biggest mistake teachers make, and it doubles your workload every single week.

**Planning** is deciding *what* you'll teach: the objective, the activity, the assessment.
**Prepping** is making the stuff: the slides, the copies, the anchor charts.

When you mix the two, a 20-minute decision turns into a 90-minute rabbit hole of fonts and Pinterest boards. During your planning sitting, resist the urge to build anything. Jot "make slide deck for fractions intro" on a prep list and keep moving. You'll batch the making later — and it'll go faster because all the decisions are already made.

## Set a Hard Container

Planning expands to fill whatever time you give it. If you start Saturday morning "whenever," it will consume whenever through Sunday night.

Instead, pick one recurring block — say, Thursday after school or Saturday at 9 a.m. — and put a hard stop on it. Set a timer for 75 minutes. The constraint sounds stressful, but it's actually freeing: your brain stops dithering over perfect options because there isn't time. Done beats perfect, and a timer enforces done.

## Start With the Non-Negotiables

Before you plan a single lesson, anchor your week. Write down:

- **The fixed events** — assemblies, early dismissals, your prep periods, that IEP meeting on Wednesday
- **The must-hit objectives** — the standards or skills that genuinely have to move forward this week
- **The assessments** — anything being checked, collected, or graded

This takes five minutes and changes everything. Now you're filling a known shape instead of staring at five blank days. You'll also stop planning beautiful Tuesday lessons for a Tuesday that turns out to be a half day.

## Batch by Subject, Not by Day

Most teachers plan chronologically: all of Monday, then all of Tuesday. It feels logical, but it forces your brain to switch contexts constantly — math brain, reading brain, science brain, back to math brain.

Try planning *all your math for the week* in one pass, then all your ELA, then everything else. Staying in one subject keeps your momentum going, and patterns emerge naturally. You might realize Monday's concept needs a quick review built into Wednesday, or that you can reuse Thursday's exit ticket format on Friday. Those connections are invisible when you're hopping subjects every fifteen minutes.

## Use One Template and Reuse It Forever

Decision fatigue is the silent killer of planning sessions. If every lesson requires you to invent a structure from scratch, you're making hundreds of unnecessary decisions.

Build one simple lesson skeleton — for example: **hook → mini-lesson → guided practice → independent work → quick check** — and use it as your default for everything. You're not being lazy; you're being smart. A predictable structure also helps your students, who thrive on routine.

Do the same at the weekly level. Maybe Mondays are always "launch the new concept," Fridays are always "review and assess." Save these templates somewhere you'll actually reuse them, and future-you gets to skip straight to the good part: the content.

## Plan to 80%, Not 100%

Here's a truth experienced teachers know in their bones: the week will not go as planned. A fire drill eats twenty minutes. The concept you thought would take one day takes two. A class discussion goes somewhere wonderful and you let it run.

If you plan every minute of every day, one hiccup derails everything and you end up "replanning" nightly — which is how planning creeps back into your evenings.

Instead, plan to about 80% capacity. Mark one block per day (or a couple per week) as a **flex block**: reteach time, extension activities, a review game, or simply breathing room. If the week goes smoothly, you always have something purposeful to fill it. If it doesn't, you absorbed the chaos without touching your evening.

## Keep a Running Idea List All Week

The blank page is the enemy of a fast planning session. Make sure you never face one.

Keep a single, always-accessible list — a note on your phone, a doc, a sticky note on your laptop — where you toss ideas the moment they appear. A colleague mentions a great discussion prompt? List. A student asks a question that would make a perfect warm-up? List. You see an activity idea while scrolling at 10 p.m.? List.

When your planning block arrives, you're not generating ideas from nothing. You're just sorting a list you built all week without trying. This one habit can easily cut your planning time by a third.

## A Quick Recap

One sitting, one system:

1. Decide only — save the prepping for later
2. Time-box it to 60–90 minutes
3. Anchor the week with fixed events and must-hit objectives
4. Plan by subject, not by day
5. Use one repeatable template
6. Plan to 80% and build in flex time
7. Collect ideas all week so you never start cold

None of this requires a single new app or purchase. It just requires a structure — and the willingness to protect it.
