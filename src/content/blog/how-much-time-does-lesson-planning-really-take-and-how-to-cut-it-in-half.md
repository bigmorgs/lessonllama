# How Much Time Does Lesson Planning Really Take? (And How to Cut It in Half)

*Photo by [Kelly Sikkema](https://unsplash.com/@kellysikkema?utm_source=lesson_llama&utm_medium=referral) on [Unsplash](https://unsplash.com/photos/white-spiral-notebook-on-brown-wooden-table-2q_frVRXWfQ?utm_source=lesson_llama&utm_medium=referral)*

It's 8:47 on a Sunday night. The dinner dishes are done (mostly), the laundry is in a heap you'll deal with later, and you're exactly where you were last Sunday: hunched over a laptop, trying to turn a vague curriculum objective into something real kids will actually engage with tomorrow morning.

If lesson planning feels like it eats an unfair share of your week, you're not imagining it — and you're definitely not alone. It's one of the most common things teachers talk about when they talk about time. So let's talk about it honestly: how long planning really takes, where the hours actually go, and what genuinely helps you get some of them back.

## So, How Much Time Does Lesson Planning Actually Take?

Ask ten teachers and you'll get ten answers, but the pattern is remarkably consistent. National surveys routinely put the average teacher workweek north of 50 hours, and somewhere between 5 and 10 of those hours go to planning and prep — often more in your first few years, or when you're handed a new course, a new grade level, or a new curriculum.

Here's the part that stings: almost none of that planning time happens during the school day. Those "planning periods" get swallowed by meetings, parent emails, make-up tests, and the student who just needs five minutes. Which means planning migrates to evenings, weekends, and the mythical land known as "summer."

If that math feels uncomfortably familiar, the next question matters more: where is all that time actually *going*?

## Where All That Planning Time Actually Goes

Most teachers aren't slow planners. They're overloaded ones. When you watch the hours disappear, they usually leak out in a few predictable places:

**The resource hunt.** You know exactly what you want to teach. Finding the *right* activity, video, or worksheet to teach it? That's forty minutes of scrolling, previewing, and discarding.

**Starting from scratch — again.** Even when you taught this exact lesson last year, the file is buried in a folder named "Unit 3 FINAL v2," so you rebuild it from memory.

**The differentiation spiral.** You plan the core lesson, then a version for your IEP students, then an extension for early finishers, then a fallback for when the tech inevitably fails.

**Perfectionism with a side of guilt.** A decent lesson takes 30 minutes to plan. A *great* one takes three hours, and it's hard to know when to stop — because these are real kids, and you care.

## Why "Just Plan Faster" Is Terrible Advice

Here's what doesn't work: someone who hasn't been in a classroom telling you to "work smarter, not harder." Speed isn't the real problem. The real problem is that planning has no natural stopping point. There's always a better video, a cleverer hook, a tighter exit ticket.

So the goal isn't to become a faster teacher. It's to put boundaries and systems around a task that will happily expand to fill every hour you give it. Here's how teachers who've cracked this actually do it.

## 7 Ways to Cut Your Planning Time (Without Cutting Corners)

**1. Timebox it — and mean it.** Decide planning gets 90 minutes tonight, not "however long it takes." Parkinson's Law is real: work expands to fill the time available. A timer creates the constraint your to-do list won't.

**2. Plan in batches, not daily.** Planning five lessons in one sitting is dramatically faster than planning one lesson five times. You build momentum, spot connections across the week, and only have to get into "planning mode" once.

**3. Use a skeleton, not a blank page.** Create one lesson template you trust — warm-up, direct instruction, guided practice, independent work, exit ticket, or whatever fits your style — and start every plan from it. Blank pages are where planning time goes to die.

**4. Build a "greatest hits" bank.** Every time a lesson works, drop it (plus a two-line note about *why* it worked) into one organized place. Next year's you will be deeply grateful. Future-you is the colleague who never lets you down.

**5. Steal like a teacher.** You do not get extra points for building everything from scratch. A solid activity from a trusted colleague or a curated resource site, adapted to your kids, beats a homemade masterpiece that cost you three hours.

**6. Plan the must-haves first.** If you ran out of time, what does tomorrow's lesson absolutely need? Nail the objective, the core activity, and one check for understanding. The rest is decoration — add it only if time allows.

**7. Protect one planning-free evening.** Pick a weeknight that is sacred. No schoolwork, no guilt. Teachers who protect their downtime don't just feel better — they plan more efficiently the rest of the week, because rest is fuel, not a reward.
