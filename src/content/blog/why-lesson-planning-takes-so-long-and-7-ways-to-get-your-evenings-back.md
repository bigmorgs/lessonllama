It's Sunday afternoon. The laptop is open, the coffee's gone cold, and somehow the weekend has narrowed down to this: you, a blank planning document, and the slow realisation that the next few hours are already spoken for.

If lesson planning regularly swallows your evenings and weekends, you're not alone — and it's not because you're slow, disorganised, or doing it wrong. Planning takes a long time for real, structural reasons. This week, we're digging into where those hours actually go, and seven practical shifts that can hand some of them back to you.

## How Long Does Lesson Planning Actually Take?

Ask any staffroom and you'll hear numbers that would shock people outside the profession. Many teachers report spending five to ten hours a week on planning and preparation — and early-career teachers often spend more. Workload surveys around the world consistently put planning and marking at the top of the list of after-hours time drains.

So if it feels like planning takes forever, that's because it genuinely does. The question isn't whether you're working hard enough. It's whether the process itself is working for you.

## Where the Time Really Goes

Before we fix it, it helps to name it. Most planning hours disappear into a handful of predictable places:

**The blank page.** Starting every lesson from scratch means every planning session begins with ten minutes of staring, scrolling, and second-guessing before anything actually gets written.

**Curriculum cross-referencing.** Whether you're mapping to the Australian Curriculum, your state standards, or a school scope and sequence, finding the right code, checking the elaborations, and making sure you're covering what you're meant to cover is fiddly, careful work.

**The resource rabbit hole.** You go looking for one worksheet and resurface forty minutes later with seventeen tabs open and nothing downloaded. Pinterest, TPT, Google — the searching can easily outlast the planning itself.

**Differentiation in your head at 9pm.** Quietly adjusting every task for the student who's struggling, the one who finishes in four minutes, and the three who'll be away. It rarely gets written down, but it takes real time and real energy.

**Reinventing last year.** You taught a brilliant lesson on this exact topic twelve months ago. Where is it? Nobody knows. So you build it again.

## 7 Practical Ways to Save Time on Lesson Planning

None of these require new software, a new job, or a personality transplant. They're small process shifts that compound over a term.

### 1. Start with the learning intention, not the activity

It's tempting to plan by hunting for a fun activity and working backwards. It's also the slowest way to plan. Decide what students need to learn first, then choose the shortest path to get there. A clear intention makes every other decision faster — and often the "perfect" activity turns out to be a simple one.

### 2. Batch your planning

Planning one lesson at a time means paying the "getting started" cost over and over again. Instead, plan in blocks: a whole week of maths in one sitting, or a full unit sequence before the term starts. Once your head is in a subject, staying there is far more efficient than switching between five different contexts in one evening.

### 3. Timebox it

Planning expands to fill the time available — give it three hours and it will take three hours. Set a timer: 45 minutes for tomorrow's lessons, full stop. You'll be surprised how often the timeboxed version is just as good as the leisurely one. Done beats perfect.

### 4. Use one template you trust

A consistent planning format — learning intention, warm-up, main task, check for understanding, wrap-up — removes dozens of micro-decisions from every session. You're not being rigid; you're saving your decision-making energy for the parts that actually need it.

### 5. Build a "greatest hits" bank

Every time a lesson works, save it somewhere you'll actually find it: a folder, a doc, a note on your phone. One line is enough — "Fractions intro with the paper-folding task, worked brilliantly with Year 5." Future you, planning the same topic next year, will be extremely grateful.

### 6. Adapt before you create

Originality is overrated at 8pm on a Tuesday. Before building anything from scratch, ask: does a version of this already exist — in my bank, from a colleague, in a resource I trust? Adapting something solid takes a fraction of the time of creating something new, and the learning is usually just as good.

### 7. Plan for the middle, adjust at the edges

Instead of designing three separate versions of every task, plan one solid core lesson and jot two quick notes: how to scaffold it down, how to extend it up. Most differentiation happens in the moment anyway. Capturing it as a two-line margin note instead of a parallel lesson plan can save an hour a week on its own.

## A Quick Word About Perfectionism

Here's the uncomfortable truth underneath a lot of planning time: many of us hold ourselves to a standard no human could sustain. Every lesson doesn't need to be a masterpiece. A good-enough lesson, taught by a rested and present teacher, beats a flawless plan taught by an exhausted one. Protecting your evenings isn't cutting corners — it's part of doing the job well for the long haul.
