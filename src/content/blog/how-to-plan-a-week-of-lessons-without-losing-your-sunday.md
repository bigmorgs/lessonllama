# How to Plan a Week of Lessons Without Losing Your Sunday

*Photo by [Kelly Sikkema](https://unsplash.com/@kellysikkema?utm_source=lesson_llama&utm_medium=referral) on [Unsplash](https://unsplash.com/photos/white-spiral-notebook-on-brown-wooden-table-2q_frVRXWfQ?utm_source=lesson_llama&utm_medium=referral)*

It's 4:15 on a Sunday afternoon. You're at the kitchen table, laptop open, surrounded by sticky notes, a half-drunk cup of coffee, and a pacing guide that's judging you. You told yourself this would take an hour. That was three hours ago.

If this scene feels uncomfortably familiar, you're not alone — and more importantly, you're not doing anything wrong. Surveys consistently show teachers spend seven or more hours a week on planning, most of it unpaid, most of it on weekends. The problem usually isn't your work ethic. It's that nobody ever taught us a *system* for weekly planning. We just kind of... improvise it, every single week, from scratch.

This post is the routine I wish someone had handed me my first year. It's not about prettier plans or color-coded binders. It's about getting a solid week of lessons on paper fast enough that Sunday still belongs to you.

## Why Lesson Planning Eats Your Weekend

Before the routine, it helps to name what's actually going wrong:

- **Planning expands to fill the time you give it.** If Sunday is your planning window, planning will take all of Sunday.
- **You're making hundreds of micro-decisions.** Every lesson is a blank page: structure, timing, materials, grouping. Decision fatigue is real, and it's exhausting.
- **You're starting from zero every week.** No template means reinventing the wheel five days in a row, per prep.
- **Perfectionism sneaks in.** Somewhere between Pinterest and your second year of teaching, "planned" started meaning "Pinterest-worthy."

The fix isn't more discipline. It's fewer decisions.

## Start with Anchors, Not Activities

Most of us plan forward: "What am I doing Monday?" Try planning backward instead.

Before you think about a single activity, ask: *What must students know or be able to do by Friday?* Then identify the two or three **must-happen moments** for each class that week — the quiz, the lab, the seminar discussion, the writing draft. Put those on the calendar first. They're your anchors.

Everything else is connective tissue. Once the anchors are placed, Monday through Thursday mostly plan themselves: you're just building toward and from the moments that matter.

This one shift cuts planning time dramatically, because you're no longer asking the infinite question ("what could I do?"). You're asking a finite one ("what gets us to Thursday's lab?").

## Batch by Block, Not by Day

Here's a mistake I made for years: planning chronologically. Monday for all my preps, then Tuesday for all my preps. It feels organized, but it forces your brain to switch contexts constantly.

Instead, **plan one prep for the whole week in a single sitting.** All of Monday-through-Friday for your first-period class, then all of it for your third-period class. Three preps means three batches.

Staying inside one subject and one group of kids keeps your momentum going. The second lesson in a batch takes half as long as the first, and by the fifth you're basically on autopilot. Chronological planning never gives you that runway.

## Build a Lesson Skeleton You Reuse Every Week

This is the single biggest time-saver on the list. Stop designing lesson structure. Pick one and reuse it.

A skeleton might look like: warm-up (5 min), mini-lesson (10–15 min), guided practice (10 min), independent work (15 min), exit ticket (5 min). Yours can be whatever fits your classroom — the point is that it's *fixed*.

Once the structure is decided, planning stops being architecture and becomes fill-in-the-blank. What's the warm-up? What's the mini-lesson? Done. You've eliminated an entire category of decisions before you sit down.

Bonus: students thrive on predictable routines. A consistent skeleton isn't just faster for you — it's calmer for them.

## Timebox Every Session

Unbounded planning becomes endless planning. So give it a container.

Pick a weekly planning window — say, Thursday after school for 90 minutes, plus 45 minutes on Friday — and defend it like a meeting with your principal. Then timebox inside it: 25 minutes per batch, timer on your phone.

When the timer goes off, you move on. This feels wrong at first. But here's the truth every veteran teacher eventually learns: **the 80% lesson delivered by a rested teacher beats the 100% lesson delivered by an exhausted one.** The timer is how you protect the rested teacher.

## Keep a Parking Lot for Ideas

Half of planning time is actually *idea-hunting* time — scrolling, searching, trying to remember that great hook you saw somewhere.

Fix it with a parking lot: one running document or notebook where you dump ideas the moment they occur to you. A video a student mentions on Tuesday. A question someone asks that would make a perfect discussion opener. A meme that explains fractions better than your textbook does.

Then, when you sit down to plan, you're not inventing — you're assembling from a pile you built all week without trying. It turns planning from creation into curation, which is much faster.

## Plan for the Week to Go Wrong

Because it will. There's an assembly Tuesday. Half your fourth period is on a field trip Thursday. The copier dies Monday morning.

So build one **flex buffer** into every week: a low-prep, high-value activity you can drop anywhere — a review game, a skill station day, a silent reading and conference block. Knowing the buffer exists means disruptions cost you a shuffle, not a full replan at 9 p.m.

## What "Good Enough" Planning Actually Looks Like

Let's define done, because vague finish lines are how Sundays disappear.

A lesson is planned when it has three things:

1. **An objective** you could say out loud in one sentence
2. **A materials list** short enough to gather in five minutes
3. **One check for understanding** — an exit ticket, a thumbs check, a quick conference

That's it. If those three exist, the lesson is planned. Everything beyond that is polish, and polish is optional on a Tuesday in October.

## The Real Goal

Here's what I want you to take from all of this: lesson planning is a *task*, not a lifestyle. It was never supposed to be the thing that eats your weekends, your evenings, and your energy for the actual humans in your classroom.

The goal isn't a beautiful plan book. It's effective teaching, done by a teacher who still has a life — and who makes it to June with something left in the tank.

A good system gets you there. Anchors, batches, skeletons, timers, a parking lot, and a buffer. Try two of them this week and see how your Sunday feels.
