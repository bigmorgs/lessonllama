# How to Reclaim Your Sundays: A Back-to-School Lesson Planning Routine That Actually Works

*Photo by [Kelly Sikkema](https://unsplash.com/@kellysikkema?utm_source=lesson_llama&utm_medium=referral) on [Unsplash](https://unsplash.com/photos/white-spiral-notebook-on-brown-wooden-table-2q_frVRXWfQ?utm_source=lesson_llama&utm_medium=referral)*

It's that time of year again. Fresh bulletin boards, newly labeled supply bins, a planner with actual blank pages in it — and that familiar Sunday evening feeling creeping in right on schedule.

You know the one. The week starts tomorrow, the lessons aren't quite planned, and suddenly your "weekend" turns into three hours at the kitchen table with a laptop, a cold cup of coffee, and forty-seven open browser tabs.

Here's the good news: the problem usually isn't you. It's the system — or the lack of one. Teachers aren't bad at planning. We're just doing it without a routine that protects our time. Let's fix that before the semester gets away from us.

## Why Lesson Planning Eats Your Whole Weekend

Before we talk solutions, it helps to name what's actually going wrong. For most teachers, weekend planning spirals for three reasons:

**Decision fatigue.** Every lesson starts from zero. What am I teaching? How am I opening it? What activity? What assessment? Multiply that by five days and multiple preps, and you've made hundreds of micro-decisions before you've even typed a learning objective.

**Perfectionism.** We plan for the lesson we *wish* we were teaching instead of the one our actual students need on a Tuesday in October.

**No container.** Planning expands to fill whatever time you give it. Give it a whole Sunday, and it will happily take the whole Sunday.

A good routine addresses all three. Here's how.

## Build a Weekly Skeleton First

The single biggest time-saver is a fixed lesson structure you reuse every day. Something like: warm-up, mini-lesson, guided practice, independent work, exit ticket. Your exact shape doesn't matter — what matters is that you stop reinventing the container.

When the structure is decided in advance, planning becomes filling in blanks instead of designing from scratch. You're only asking "what content goes in the mini-lesson on Wednesday?" instead of "what is Wednesday?"

Spend one hour this week building your skeleton. It's the highest-leverage hour of planning you'll do all year.

## Batch by Subject, Not by Day

Most teachers plan Monday, then Tuesday, then Wednesday. It feels logical, but it forces your brain to switch contexts constantly — math brain, then ELA brain, then science brain, all in one sitting.

Try planning all of one subject for the whole week in a single block instead. When you stay in "math mode" for an hour, you build momentum. You notice connections across the week. Activities get stronger because you can see the full arc instead of isolated days.

Even if you teach multiple sections of the same subject, batching by prep rather than by day will cut your planning time noticeably.

## Reuse Ruthlessly

Somewhere along the way, a lot of us absorbed the idea that good teachers create everything fresh. That's nonsense, and it's a fast track to burnout.

Dig up last year's lessons. Keep a "greatest hits" folder — digital or physical — of activities that actually worked. Pull from colleagues, from shared drives, from that binder you swore you'd organize in June.

Reusing isn't laziness. It's how you get better. The second time you teach a lesson, you improve it. The third time, it's genuinely great. Teachers who reinvent everything every year never get to version three of anything.

A useful rule of thumb: aim to reuse or adapt at least 60% of what you teach, and save your creative energy for the 40% that really needs it.

## Plan Two Weeks Out — Not the Whole Year

August ambition is real. We sit down with a pacing guide and map out beautiful, detailed plans through Thanksgiving.

Then a fire drill, an assembly, and a surprise unit on reteaching multiplication happen, and the whole thing is obsolete by September 15th.

Detailed planning works best in a two-week window. Beyond that, keep things loose — topics and rough pacing only. This isn't giving up on long-range planning; it's matching your detail level to what you can actually predict. You'll waste far less work, and your plans will reflect your real students instead of your imaginary ones.

## Timebox Your Planning

Remember how planning expands to fill the time available? Flip that around. Give it a container.

Pick a specific block — say, 90 minutes on Sunday or two afternoons after school — set a timer, and plan only inside that window. When the timer goes off, you're done. Whatever isn't planned gets simplified, not extended.

This feels uncomfortable at first. But here's what happens: your brain, faced with a real constraint, stops fussing over font choices and starts prioritizing what actually matters. Most teachers who timebox find they plan the same amount of learning in half the time — because the extra hours were never about learning. They were about anxiety.

## Know Your "Good Enough" Line

Not every lesson needs to be a masterpiece. Honestly, most lessons need to be clear, purposeful, and connected to the one before it.

Try asking yourself one question when you're deep in the weeds on a plan: *Will this detail change what students learn tomorrow?* A well-chosen problem set? Yes. Color-coding your agenda slide? Almost certainly no.

Draw your "good enough" line before you start planning, not after. Decide what a solid Tuesday lesson looks like in your classroom, and let that be the standard. Save the showpiece lessons for once a unit, when they'll actually land.
